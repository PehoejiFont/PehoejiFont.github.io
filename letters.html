How the computer stores POJ letters

To a computer, o͘ is not one letter. It is a letter plus a sticker.

Letters and stickers

Every character on a computer has a number in a big world-wide list called Unicode. The letter o is number U+006F, for example.

Some accented letters have their own number, such as ó (U+00F3). We call these ready-made letters.

Others are built from a plain letter plus one or more stickers. Unicode calls these stickers combining marks. The sticker has no letter of its own; it attaches to the letter in front of it.

oU+006F +  ͘U+0358 = o͘
aU+0061 +  ̍U+030D = a̍

POJ needs two stickers that European languages don't use:

  • the dot at the top right of o͘ (U+0358), and
  • the short vertical line for the 8th tone, as in chia̍h (U+030D).

Many fonts simply don't have these two. That is the first reason POJ breaks.

Will Unicode ever add a ready-made o͘? No. When POJ was added in 2005, Unicode chose to add the dot sticker instead, and its rules no longer allow new ready-made accented letters. Stickers are the only way, now and in the future.

Two stickers at once

A toned o͘ carries two stickers: the tone on top and the dot at the right. The font has to place both without them bumping into each other.

oU+006F+  ́U+0301+  ͘U+0358 =ó͘

The same letter, written different ways

Here is the surprising part. ó͘ can reach the font in at least three different codes, and they all should look the same:

1. Ready-made ó + dot (most web pages and phones)

óU+00F3+  ͘U+0358

2. Plain o + tone + dot (Mac file names, text copied out of PDFs)

oU+006F+  ́U+0301+  ͘U+0358

3. Plain o + dot + tone (some keyboards, where you type o͘ first, then the tone)

oU+006F+  ͘U+0358+  ́U+0301

Unicode says all three mean the same thing, and it has a standard “tidy-up” that turns them into one form (form 1 above; experts call it NFC). After tidying, the tone always comes before the dot.

Chrome, Firefox and Android tidy the text before the font sees it. Other programs may not, so a good font is built to handle every form itself.

Why you might care: if a document mixes forms, searching for hō͘ may miss some of them. Tools that tidy text to NFC fix this.

The i must drop its dot

When a tone sits on i, the dot of the i should disappear, just like in í. Ready-made letters such as í already do this. But i̍ (8th tone) is always built from stickers, so the font has to remove the dot.

Correctchi̍t (一) in Lesson One: no dot under the line
Wrongthe same word in Taigi Huninn POJ (字咍): the dot stays, under the tone line

The small ⁿ is a letter, not a sticker

The nasal ⁿ in thiⁿ (天) is a letter of its own (U+207F). Fonts draw it easily, but two things go wrong with it:

  • Some “clean-up” tools turn it into a plain n. A different tidy-up called NFKC (used by some search engines and databases) changes thiⁿ into thin. Use NFC, never NFKC, for Taiwanese text.
  • It has no capital. Writing in ALL CAPS gives THIⁿ. Some people use ᴺ (U+1D3A) or NN instead.

Look-alikes that are not POJ

Before the dot sticker existed, people used whatever was on the keyboard. You will still find these in old text:

If you have old text like this, convert it to the real stickers once, and it will work everywhere.

Hyphens

POJ joins syllables with - and marks the neutral tone with two hyphens: tńg--khì (轉去, go back). Some word processors “helpfully” turn -- into a long dash (–). Turn that auto-correct option off.
